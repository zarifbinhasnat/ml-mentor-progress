# Day 86 — Concept 86: Classifier-Free Guidance

## 🧠 CONCEPT OF THE DAY

**Intuition.** Imagine two GPS voices in the car. One only knows the local road network (the *unconditional* model — "generic plausible image"). The other also knows your destination (the *conditional* model — "an image of a cat"). If you just follow the destination-aware voice, you get to a cat, eventually. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) says: don't just follow it — take the *difference* between what the destination-aware voice tells you and what the generic voice tells you, and steer **more** in that direction than the destination-aware voice itself suggests. You overshoot past "a cat" toward "an unmistakable, unambiguous cat," trading route diversity for route confidence.

**The math.** Train a *single* network to do both jobs by randomly dropping the conditioning during training (e.g. 10–20% of the time, replace the class/text embedding $c$ with a learned null token $\varnothing$):

$$\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) \quad \text{and} \quad \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing)$$

come from the *same* weights. At sampling time, combine them:

$$\hat\epsilon = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing) + w\big(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing)\big)$$

Equivalently in score form, $\hat s = (1-w)\, s_{\text{uncond}} + w\, s_{\text{cond}}$ — a straight-line extrapolation through the two scores. $w=0$ is pure unconditional generation, $w=1$ recovers ordinary conditional sampling, and $w>1$ (typically 3–15 in practice) pushes *past* the conditional score, implicitly sampling from a distribution proportional to $p(x|c)^w \, p(x)^{1-w}$ — a "sharpened," lower-entropy version of $p(x|c)$ that trades sample diversity for how strongly the output obeys the prompt.

**Why it matters / where it leads.** Before CFG, guidance meant training a *separate* noisy-image classifier and adding $\nabla_x \log p_\phi(c|x)$ to the score (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) — extra network, extra training run, and vulnerable to the classifier being "fooled" by adversarial-looking artifacts in a way that doesn't actually improve image quality. CFG (Ho & Salimans, 2022) gets the same steering effect from a single model with a training-time coin flip, which is why it's now the default in essentially every diffusion system you'll touch — and why every text-to-image API exposes a `guidance_scale` knob that's just $w$. Tomorrow you'll see CFG deployed inside the full production recipe: latent diffusion, where this same steered sampling happens in a VAE's compressed latent space instead of pixel space.

**Interview question:** *Why does classifier-free guidance require training one model with conditioning dropout, instead of just training two separate models — one conditional, one unconditional — and combining their outputs at inference time?*

## 🐍 PYTHONIC EDGE

CFG needs two forward passes per denoising step (conditional + unconditional). The naive way calls the model twice; the idiomatic way batches them into one call — half the Python-level overhead, and it lets the GPU parallelize both passes instead of running them back-to-back.

```python
import torch

# ---- BAD: two separate forward passes ----
def cfg_step_bad(model, x_t, t, cond_emb, uncond_emb, w):
    eps_cond = model(x_t, t, cond_emb)      # calling model(...) invokes __call__, which wraps forward() with hooks -- never call .forward() directly
    eps_uncond = model(x_t, t, uncond_emb)  # second, fully separate kernel launch / graph traversal
    return eps_uncond + w * (eps_cond - eps_uncond)

# ---- CLEAN: one batched forward pass ----
def cfg_step(model, x_t, t, cond_emb, uncond_emb, w):
    x_in = torch.cat([x_t, x_t], dim=0)             # dim= is a keyword-only-by-convention arg; concatenates along the batch axis
    t_in = torch.cat([t, t], dim=0)
    c_in = torch.cat([uncond_emb, cond_emb], dim=0)  # order matters: uncond first, matches the chunk() unpacking below

    eps = model(x_in, t_in, c_in)                    # ONE forward call, batch size doubled instead of two separate calls
    eps_uncond, eps_cond = eps.chunk(2, dim=0)        # tuple unpacking: chunk() returns a tuple of views, split in declaration order

    return eps_uncond + w * (eps_cond - eps_uncond)   # w > 1 is legal here -- extrapolation, not interpolation
```

`eps.chunk(2, dim=0)` returns *views* into `eps`, not copies — no `.clone()` needed since we only read from them. This "stack the branches, one pass, split the output" pattern generalizes beyond CFG: any time you'd naively call the same model N times with different conditioning, check whether you can fold N into the batch dimension instead.

## 📡 SIGNAL LAB

The CFG formula $\hat\epsilon = \epsilon_{\text{uncond}} + w(\epsilon_{\text{cond}} - \epsilon_{\text{uncond}})$ has an exact DSP twin: **unsharp masking**, the classical image-sharpening operator.

$$\text{sharpened} = \text{blurred} + w\,(\text{original} - \text{blurred})$$

Swap "blurred (low-pass)" $\leftrightarrow$ "unconditional score" and "original" $\leftrightarrow$ "conditional score," and it's the *same* linear extrapolation-past-a-baseline operator, just applied in pixel space instead of score space:

![Left: CFG guided score extrapolating past the conditional score as w increases, showing a growing discontinuity near x=2. Right: classical unsharp masking on a rectangular pulse, showing ringing/overshoot at the edges growing with w, using the identical algebraic formula.](graph_DAY_86.png)

**Worked insight.** In the right panel, `blurred` is a Gaussian low-pass of a clean pulse — literally a smaller-bandwidth version of the signal, the pixel-domain analogue of the smoother, less-committal unconditional score. `sharpened = blurred + w*(original - blurred)` boosts the *residual* (original minus blurred) — which is dominated by high spatial frequencies at the pulse's edges. As $w$ grows past 1, you don't just recover the sharp edge, you **overshoot it**: classic unsharp-mask ringing/halo artifacts, visible as the spikes exceeding the original pulse's amplitude at $w=3, 7$.

**So what.** This is exactly what's happening in the left panel too: the CFG score doesn't just sharpen $p(x|c)$, it develops an increasingly steep, near-discontinuous transition right at the conditional mode as $w$ grows — the score-space equivalent of a ringing overshoot. This is the mechanistic reason high-guidance-scale diffusion samples look "oversaturated" and over-contrasted, and it's a genuinely useful diagnostic lens for your research lane: an image generated at unusually high $w$ should carry a disproportionate boost in a specific high-frequency band relative to natural images or low-$w$ generations — the same signature an over-aggressive unsharp mask leaves in a PSD. If you're building a synthetic-image detector, guidance scale is a hyperparameter of the *generator* that leaves a frequency fingerprint, not just a sampling knob.

## 🏋️ THE GAUNTLET

**Merge K Sorted Lists**

You're given `k` singly-linked lists, each already sorted in ascending order. Merge all of them into one sorted linked list and return its head.

**Constraints:**
- Total number of nodes across all lists: `0 <= n <= 10^4`
- `0 <= k <= 10^4`
- `-10^4 <= Node.val <= 10^4`
- Target: **O(n log k)** time.

**Hints (escalating):**
1. Merging the lists two-at-a-time in a naive left-to-right chain re-scans earlier, already-merged nodes on every pass — that's what pushes it toward $O(nk)$ in the worst case. You want to always know the smallest *current* candidate across all $k$ lists without re-comparing all $k$ heads every single time.
2. You need a structure that gives you the minimum of up to $k$ candidates in $O(\log k)$, and lets you swap one candidate out for "the next node from that same list" in $O(\log k)$ too — that's a min-heap over list heads, not a scan.
3. Push the head of each non-null list into the heap once at the start. Then repeatedly: pop the minimum, append it to your output, and — if that popped node has a `next` — push `next` back in. The heap never holds more than $k$ nodes at once, and every one of the $n$ nodes is pushed and popped exactly once.

**Pattern:** k-way merge via a min-heap (`priority_queue` in C++). **Target complexity:** $O(n \log k)$ time, $O(k)$ space.

---

## 🏗️ BLUEPRINT

CFG's inference cost is not free: computing $\hat\epsilon$ needs *two* forward passes (or one batch-doubled pass) at **every** denoising step, so it's a flat 2× latency/compute tax on top of whatever sampler step count you're already paying. Production serving stacks either eat that tax directly, or amortize it away with **guidance distillation** — train a *second*, smaller student model to directly predict the already-guided $\hat\epsilon$ for a fixed (or embedded) $w$, folding the 2× runtime cost into a one-time training cost instead of paying it on every single inference call. The tradeoff: distillation locks in (or narrows) the guidance scale you can serve at, so it's a latency-vs-flexibility knob, not a free lunch.

## 🔓 GAUNTLET SOLUTION

```cpp
#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

struct ListNode {
    int val;
    ListNode* next;
    ListNode(int x) : val(x), next(nullptr) {}
};

struct Cmp {
    bool operator()(ListNode* a, ListNode* b) {
        return a->val > b->val; // reversed comparator -> priority_queue behaves as a min-heap
    }
};

ListNode* mergeKLists(vector<ListNode*>& lists) {
    priority_queue<ListNode*, vector<ListNode*>, Cmp> pq;

    for (ListNode* node : lists) {
        if (node) pq.push(node);
    }

    ListNode dummy(0);
    ListNode* tail = &dummy;

    while (!pq.empty()) {
        ListNode* node = pq.top();
        pq.pop();
        tail->next = node;
        tail = node;
        if (node->next) pq.push(node->next);
    }

    tail->next = nullptr;
    return dummy.next;
}
```

Every one of the `n` nodes is pushed and popped from a heap of size at most `k` exactly once, giving $O(n \log k)$ time and $O(k)$ auxiliary space for the heap.

## 💡 CONCEPT ANSWER

Two separately trained models have independently initialized weights, independent training trajectories, and no shared feature space — nothing guarantees that $\epsilon_{\text{cond}}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{uncond}}$ "line up" as two evaluations of a single, smoothly varying function of the conditioning. Their difference, $\epsilon_{\text{cond}} - \epsilon_{\text{uncond}}$, would then be dominated by incidental discrepancies between the two networks (different noise floors, different biases, different failure modes) rather than by a clean, meaningful "what does the condition add" signal — and extrapolating along a noisy, poorly-aligned direction (especially at $w \gg 1$) tends to amplify whatever's inconsistent between the two models into visible artifacts. Conditioning dropout instead trains *one* shared network, so $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c)$ and $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)$ are the same function evaluated at two nearby points in conditioning space (real embedding vs. a learned null embedding) — their difference is a much better-behaved, locally consistent estimate of the conditioning's effect, extrapolates more gracefully, and comes at zero extra parameter or serving cost versus training and hosting two full models.
